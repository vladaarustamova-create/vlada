THE METHOD — DAY ONE

How memory turns words into places

Japanese study traditions and old mnemonic tricks, combined into one daily routine for three languages.

KEY TAKEAWAYS

●Learn little but every day: a short daily habit beats long weekend sessions. ●Turn every new word into a picture or a story — the stranger the image, the longer it lasts. ●Review on a schedule, read aloud, write by hand, and reflect for one minute after each session.

Mnemonics work because the brain remembers images and places far better than abstract sounds. When き becomes a key, when Arabic ثلاثة (three) becomes three dots on a letter, when alcalde reveals an old Arabic judge, the word stops being noise and becomes a story. Japanese education adds discipline to that imagination: Kaizen keeps the habit small enough to never skip, Shu-Ha-Ri tells you when to copy and when to create, and Ondoku — reading aloud — makes sure every word passes through the mouth and the ear.

              العربية — AL-ʿARABIYYA · A0 → A2

              ض

              Arabic is written right-to-left, has 28 letters, and most letters change shape depending on whether they are at the start, middle or end of a word. Short vowels are usually not written. Six letters (ا د ذ ر ز و) never join the letter after them.

              Good news for a Spanish speaker

              Castilian Spanish already contains several “difficult” Arabic sounds:

              • خ kh = the j of jamón
              • ث th = the z of zapato
              • ذ dh = the soft d of nada
              • غ gh = the soft g of agua
              • ر r = the tapped r of pero

                  EL PUENTE — الجسر · Spanish ↔ Arabic

                  SPANISHCARRIES FOURthousandARABIC WORDSINSIDEIT.

                  From 711 to 1492 Arabic was spoken across much of the Iberian Peninsula. Spanish kept thousands of its words — for food, irrigation, building, science and government. Many still carry the Arabic article al- glued to the front: al-makhzan became almacén. And here is the secret: these Spanish words teach you a real rule of Arabic grammar — the sun and moon letters.

                  Sun letters ☀ & moon letters ☾

                  Before 14 “sun letters” the l of ال melts into the next sound: al-sukkar is said as-sukkar → Spanish azúcar, no l! Before the other 14 “moon letters” the l stays: al-qāḍī → alcalde.

                  So when a Spanish word starts with a- + doubled-looking consonant (azúcar, arroz, aceite, aduana), think sun letter. When it starts with al- (almohada, algodón), think moon letter.
